What happened, and why the model matters.

Through 2025, LetsVPN earned its reputation honestly: independent testing had it connecting through the Great Firewall far more reliably than the big Western brands, with a dead-simple app and trip-sized pricing. Then came the spring 2026 blocking campaign. After roughly three weeks of escalating disruption its team said it was adjusting to "hourly," the company announced on April 28, 2026 that it could not continue operations targeting mainland China.

The lesson isn't that LetsVPN was bad at this. It's that any service routing a huge user base through company-operated endpoints hands the censor a finite, findable list of targets. When the pressure ratcheted up, the list got exhausted.

Traveler's VPN is built on the opposite structure. Each user gets a private server, provisioned on demand, with an ordinary cloud IP address that no one else uses and no blocklist knows. There's no central fleet whose discovery kills service for everyone at once. On top of that sits the routing engine LetsVPN's Auto Mode gestured at, done properly: per-destination rules that keep local apps direct and fast while Instagram, WhatsApp, Gmail, and YouTube tunnel, on iPhone, iPad, and native Mac.

And the trust equation is simpler. LetsVPN asked users to trust an anonymous operator with a closed protocol and a logging-permissive policy. With Traveler's VPN there's no account to create, purchases run through Apple, the tunnel is standard open protocols via sing-box, and in BYO mode your traffic never touches infrastructure we operate at all.

Questions about LetsVPN and what replaces it.

Is LetsVPN shut down completely?

No. The April 2026 announcement covered its mainland China operations, and the company said it would refund affected users. The app remains available on international App Stores. But if your reason for using it was China connectivity, that's the part that ended.

Why would a private server survive where LetsVPN didn't?

Blocking works by identifying endpoints. A commercial VPN's servers carry thousands of users and known traffic signatures, so they're worth finding and blocking, and once found, everyone on them goes dark. A private server is one ordinary cloud IP carrying one user's traffic over standard protocols. There's nothing to put on a blocklist until it's observed, and no one else's blocking takes you down. No approach is guaranteed against the firewall, but the private model removes the shared single point of failure.

Was LetsVPN safe to use?

Independent reviewers consistently flagged the same concerns: an operator with no public identity or jurisdiction, a privacy policy permitting device and usage logging, no kill switch, and no independent audit. The common framing was "fine for casual browsing, don't do banking on it." Traveler's VPN takes the opposite posture: no account, App Store purchase protections, standard open protocols, and no shared infrastructure aggregating your traffic with strangers'.
